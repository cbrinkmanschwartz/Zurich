# Source 2: Bend (2018), *The Home Office and Public Disturbance, c.1800–1832*

**Citation:** Nathan Ashley Bend, 'The Home Office and Public Disturbance, c.1800–1832' (unpublished PhD thesis, University of Hertfordshire, 2018). AHRC-funded; supervisors Paul Carter, Robert Poole, Katrina Navickas. 295 pp.
**Online:** https://uhra.herts.ac.uk/id/eprint/16880/
**Read:** chapters 1 and 3–5, appendices, archive bibliography, plus a keyword sweep for 1790s, barracks and military series. Chapters 6–8 (1811–32: Luddites, Peterloo, Swing) skipped as out of period.

## Fit to the project

- **Period:** starts c.1800, so it only touches our 1790s core through the food riots of 1800–01 (ch. 5) and its look back at 1795–96.
- **Main value:** how the Home Office handled incoming letters, and therefore what HO 42 does and does not capture. This is the most useful material yet on whether HO 42 can serve as the core measure of requests for troops.
- **Not in it:** no counts of requests for troops, no event list, nothing on barracks beyond one secondary citation.

## Findings that bear on the HO 42 strategy

### 1. HO 43 is an index to HO 42, not just background
- Bend's method (pp. 21–2): **use the HO 43 entry books to find requests.** Each reply in HO 43 gives the date of the letter it answers, which leads back to the original in HO 42. He then sampled HO 42 boxes only for the busiest periods.
- He found **replies in HO 43 whose incoming letter cannot be found** in HO 42. Letters have been lost or misplaced, including by historians.
- **Consequence:** HO 43 is a second route to requests, and a check on how complete HO 42 is.
  - A reply such as "the troops you requested have been ordered…" or "His Grace does not think it necessary to send troops…" records a request even when the original is lost.
  - This revises my earlier advice that HO 43 is background only.
  - HO 43 volumes are indexed, so they are much faster to scan than the HO 42 boxes.
- Bend's own estimate of the incoming correspondence: **over 247 boxes, at about 200–500 folios each.**

### 2. Letters carry their own response notes (useful for the Response columns)
How a letter was processed (pp. 55–6):
1. The permanent under-secretary read the incoming letter.
2. A corner was turned down and **the instruction for the reply was written on it**.
3. The letter was then **docketed on the reverse**: writer, date sent, **date of reply**, and a summary of contents.

So many HO 42 originals record the outcome themselves; our Lamberhurst row is an example. **Action:** photograph the dorse (back) of every letter. The Response columns can often be filled without HO 43.

### 3. HO 42 is a selected record of requests (the selection problem, confirmed)
- **Reporting was voluntary.** Magistrates were "encouraged but not obliged" to report disturbances (p. 79).
- **Which letters went to London** (citing Wells, *Wretched Faces*, pp. 92–3):
  - Reports went mainly when the writer **wanted assistance**.
  - Magistrates who gave in to the crowd, or handled things locally, often did not write, because central involvement was embarrassing.
  - **Good for us:** HO 42 should be rich in requests specifically.
  - **Bad for us:** it misses riots that never produced a request, so it cannot give a riot denominator.
- **Local forces needed no Home Office involvement** (p. 84). Volunteers, yeomanry and militia could be called out through the Lord Lieutenant or sheriff. Requests to the Home Office are therefore mostly for **regular troops, after local forces were judged inadequate**.
- **The Home Office checked inadequacy before sending regulars** (p. 126; HO 43/12 p. 21, Portland to Edward Greathead, 20 Jun 1800). Normal practice was to confirm with the magistrates that the volunteer corps on the spot could not keep the peace. Our "Civil power inadequate?" column is coding something the Home Office itself tested.
- **In a crisis the Home Office was bypassed** (p. 85). Magistrates were told to apply **directly to the district military commander**. Those requests leave no HO 42 trace, and should show up in the district commanders' and Horse Guards' papers.
- **Some troops were sent without any request** (pp. 125–7):
  - Oxford, September 1800: troops were sent on a clergyman's letter.
  - Birmingham, November 1800: troops were sent on Lord Lieutenants' reports.
  - In both cases the magistrates objected.
  - **For the analysis:** not every deployment follows a request. Some go against local wishes, and the Oxford corporation even refused troops.
- **Some contacts left no paper.** The Home Office and Horse Guards were almost next door, and some troop arrangements were made in person (p. 23).

### 4. How a granted request was carried out (p. 85)
1. The Home Office sent a note to **Horse Guards** (the Commander-in-Chief).
2. Horse Guards consulted the Adjutant-General and Quartermaster-General.
3. The **district commanding officer** was ordered to move troops.

- **Where to look:**
  - Commander-in-Chief out-letters (**WO 3**, to check)
  - The Secretary at War's marching orders (**WO 5**)
  - **HO 50** (example: HO 50/388 contains the Oxford 1800 exchange with the Secretary at War)
- **Cross-check with our log:** the Lamberhurst docket says "Copy… sent to Ld Amherst". Amherst was Commander-in-Chief until early 1795, which fits this process.

### 5. Gaps in HO 42, especially 1793–94
- **War-related originals left with Dundas** (p. 70). When Dundas moved from Home Secretary to Secretary for War in July 1794, original letters on war business went with him and Under-Secretary Evan Nepean. Only précis (summaries) stayed at the Home Office.
  - Bend's précis books from these years: **HO 42/216, HO 99/13, HO 102/61, HO 50/454**.
  - **Check:** whether military letters from 1793–94 (volunteer corps, militia, troop movements) survive in HO 42 only as précis, with the originals in the War Department records (WO 1, to check).
- **Some official letters left with the Home Secretaries** (pp. 67–8). Private-but-official letters often left office with the Home Secretary, so some are now in private papers:
  - **Portland Papers**, University of Nottingham: Pw V 111 (1797–1801, includes food riots) and Pw F 7201 (1794)
  - Sidmouth (Addington) Papers, Devon Heritage Centre, 152M (1812 onwards, out of period)
- **Private and secret letters** were kept in a separate series, **HO 79**, from 1798.
- **Archive structure** (ch. 3):
  - HO 42 holds all domestic in-letters, disturbances included, **until 1812**.
  - HO 40 was split off in 1812 as a *selection* prepared for the secret committees.
  - HO 41 (disturbance replies) starts in 1815–16.
  - **Before 1815–16, replies are only in HO 43.**
  - So for the 1790s, HO 42 plus HO 43 is the whole Home Office series; HO 40 and HO 41 are irrelevant, which confirms our earlier view.

## Key cases and quotes (1800–01, outside the current log window)
- **Oxford, Sep 1800**
  - Rev. David Hughes, Jesus College, warns of pamphlets inciting price-fixing. Portland sends the 11th Light Dragoons without consulting the magistrates.
  - Town Clerk W.E. Taunton protests to the Secretary at War (HO 50/388) that the dragoons insult the volunteer corps.
  - The volunteers "would scarcely exert themselves against a mob, whose cry was for lowering the Price of Provisions" (HO 42/51 ff. 36–7, 59–60).
  - Gaol riot; Willoughby reports a crowd of "50 to 200" nightly (HO 42/51 ff. 338–41).
  - **Use for the analysis:** a refusal of troops, and evidence that local forces were unreliable in food riots.
- **Birmingham, Nov 1800:** troops sent on the Lord Lieutenant's information without asking the magistrates (HO 43/12 pp. 313–6; HO 42/53 ff. 404–5).
- **Gloucestershire, Sep 1800:** Earl of Berkeley reports letters between manufacturing districts on lowering wheat prices, and restraint "till… absolutely necessary as we do not desire to create any alarm by sending for soldiers" (HO 42/51 ff. 168–9). A reluctance to request troops is itself relevant.
- **Bilston, May 1800:** Rev. Alexander Haden refuses to keep the peace "at the hazard of" his "Life, for the enriching of one part of the Community" (HO 42/50 ff. 48–9). Magistrates sympathised with the crowd.
- **Seaford / Newhaven, Apr 1795:** about 400 Oxfordshire Militia mutiny and force down food prices; put down by the Royal Horse Artillery and yeomanry (Wells, *Wretched Faces*, pp. 100–4). **Relevant to our question 5** (militias and volunteers): auxiliary forces could side with food rioters.
- **Barracks:** E.P. Thompson counts **155 barracks built 1792–1815** (*Making*, p. 663) as evidence of a nation "held down by force". Bruce Collins (*War and Empire*, 2014, p. 397) argues they reflect wartime expansion and the threat of invasion. **Both sides should be cited in any barracks argument.**
